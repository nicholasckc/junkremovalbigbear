How to Get Rid of an Old Hot Tub in Big Bear

How to get rid of an old hot tub in Big Bear: disconnecting, draining, cut-down, tight deck access, county disposal rules and cost factors.

How does hot tub removal work in Big Bear?

First an electrician or spa technician disconnects the power and plumbing. Then the spa is drained, cut into sections, carried off the deck and hauled for disposal. On Big Bear's tiered decks and tight lots, cut-down is usually how a spa gets out. County disposal rules matter: liquids aren't accepted, pieces over 4 feet long aren't accepted at transfer stations, and treated-wood surrounds can only go to the county's Mid-Valley and Victorville sites. Cost depends on spa size, deck access and cut-down complexity, so we quote each tub with a free on-site look.

Big Bear is hard on spas. Hard freezes crack plumbing that wasn't winterized, snow loads stress covers and cabinets, and vacation-rental tubs run almost nonstop through peak season. When one finally gives out, it's the heaviest thing on the property, usually sitting on a deck that was built around it. This guide covers how removal works, what the county rules mean for a spa, and what drives the cost.

Step-by-step: hot tub removal

  1. Have the power and plumbing disconnected

    A hot tub usually runs on a dedicated high-amperage circuit. A qualified electrician or spa technician should shut it off at the breaker, disconnect the spa and make the wiring safe. Any hard-plumbed fill line gets shut off and capped. This comes before everything else.

  2. Drain it completely

    Pump or siphon the water out and leave the cover open so the shell dries. County disposal sites don't accept liquids, and a spa with water in the footwell and plumbing is far heavier. Ask your water or sewer provider where treated spa water can go. Don't run it toward the lake, a storm drain or a neighbor's lot.

  3. Check the route out

    Measure gates, stair widths and railings, and look at the slope. Most mountain decks were built around the spa, so it rarely leaves the way it came in. Clear snow and ice from the whole route, because a tub removal is not a storm-day job.

  4. Remove the cover, steps and surround

    The cover, steps and any skirting or surround come off first. Wood surrounds and deck framing are often pressure-treated. Keep that lumber separate, because the Big Bear Transfer Station won't take it.

  5. Cut the shell and cabinet into sections

    The cabinet frame and shell are cut into pieces two people can carry. For self-haul to a county transfer station, pieces must be under 4 feet long. The pump and heater pack come out as one unit.

  6. Carry out, haul and sort

    Sections go down the stairs or path to the truck. The shell and cabinet go as bulky waste, metal parts and the equipment pack as metal or e-waste, and treated lumber to a site that accepts it.

  7. Inspect the deck and finish the site

    With the tub gone, check the deck boards and framing under the footprint. Years of splash and snowmelt rot wood there first. Sweep the area and cap or label the disconnected circuit for the next owner or spa.

Disposal rules for a spa in San Bernardino County

  • No liquids: county sites don't accept liquids. Drain fully and let it dry.
  • Under 4 feet: transfer stations don't accept items over 4 feet long (landfills take up to 10 feet). A whole spa isn't going into the Big Bear Transfer Station.
  • Treated wood: accepted only at the county's Mid-Valley and Victorville sites, and treated wood can't go in the trash in California.
  • Chemicals: leftover chlorine, bromine and pH chemicals are household hazardous waste. Take them to the county HHW program.
  • Uncovered loads: double fee at county sites, so tarp and strap.

Details and addresses are in the Big Bear dump and transfer station guide.

What affects the price

We don't publish prices, and phone estimates for hot tubs are guesses. These are the factors we look at on-site:

  • Size: a 2-person tub is a different job from an 8-person spa or a swim spa.
  • Access: a ground-level patio vs. a third-tier deck above a slope, gate widths, stair count and the distance to where the truck can park.
  • Cut-down complexity: acrylic shells cut quickly. Older wood-cabinet tubs and spas set into a deck take longer.
  • Surrounds and decking: a built-in surround or deck section adds tear-out and treated-wood disposal. See shed and deck tear-outs.
  • Season: snow and ice on the route add time; spring through fall is easier.
  • Disposal fees: county fees are weight-based (see the county fee table).

For vacation rental owners

A dead hot tub on an active listing costs bookings every week it sits there. We schedule tear-outs between guest stays, coordinate access through your property manager or lockbox, and send photos when the deck is clear. Replacing rather than removing? We can haul the old one the same day the new spa is delivered. Pair it with the turnover checklist to reset the whole deck.

After the tub: the deck and the lot

The footprint under a spa is usually the worst wood on the deck. If boards are soft, plan repairs before you set anything heavy there again. If you're clearing the deck and yard for fire season or a sale, see decluttering before you sell or rent and weed abatement.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to disconnect the hot tub before removal?

Yes. A qualified electrician or spa technician should disconnect the power and cap any plumbing first. After that, Junk Removal Big Bear handles the cut-down, carry-out and disposal.

Can I take a hot tub to the Big Bear dump?

Not whole. San Bernardino County transfer stations don't accept items over 4 feet long or liquids, so a spa must be drained and cut into sections first. Treated-wood surrounds can only go to the county's Mid-Valley and Victorville sites.

Can you remove a hot tub from a second-story or tiered deck?

Yes. Tight-access removals are normal in Big Bear. The tub is cut into sections and hand-carried down stairs and narrow paths, with no crane for most lots.

How much does hot tub removal cost in Big Bear?

It depends on spa size, deck access, cut-down complexity and any surround or decking that comes out with it. We give a free on-site quote with a firm price, or a quick answer from photos by text.

What do I do with leftover spa chemicals?

Chlorine, bromine and other spa chemicals are household hazardous waste. Take them to the San Bernardino County Fire HHW program, not the trash or a drain. We don't haul chemicals.

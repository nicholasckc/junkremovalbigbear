After a technician disconnects power and plumbing, the spa is drained, cut into sections, carried off the deck, and hauled for disposal — typically a half-day job on Big Bear's tiered decks and tight lots. Why Big Bear kills hot tubs

At 6,750+ feet, spas work harder than they do down the hill: hard freezes crack plumbing that wasn't winterized, snow loads stress covers and cabinets, and vacation-rental tubs run near-continuously through peak season. When one finally gives out, it becomes the heaviest piece of furniture on the property — 400 to 900 pounds dry — sitting on a deck that was built around it.

The removal process, step by step

  • 1. Disconnect: your electrician or spa tech kills the power (usually a 220V circuit) and caps the plumbing — required before any removal
  • 2. Drain: fully drained and left open to dry if possible
  • 3. Cut-down: most mountain tubs can't leave the way they arrived — decks got railed in, gates narrowed, trees grew — so we cut the shell into carryable sections
  • 4. Carry-out: sections and equipment pack are carried down stairs and switchback paths to the truck
  • 5. Haul & dispose: shell, cabinet, cover and pack are hauled the same visit, with metals recycled
  • 6. Deck sweep: the footprint is swept clean — ready for a new spa or your new deck furniture

What affects the price

Three things, mostly: size (a 2-person tub is a different job than an 8-person swim spa), access (ground-level patio vs. a third-tier deck above a slope), and cut-down complexity (acrylic shells cut fast; older wood-cabinet and jetted concrete-adjacent installs take longer). Because these vary so much lot to lot in Big Bear, phone estimates for hot tubs are guesses — an on-site look isn't.
